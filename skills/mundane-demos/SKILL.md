# mundane-demos

The Muse launch never led with benchmark scores. It led with negotiating a
bill, selling a car, booking a trip, auditing subscriptions — jobs the
viewer did last week. Mundane utility converts; benchmarks impress people
who were already convinced.

## Pick the right task

- **The viewer did it this morning.** Email triage, calendar Tetris, expense
  reports, subscription audits. Recognition creates desire: "I hate doing
  that" becomes "I want that."
- **One job, end to end.** Not a feature tour. The bill is negotiated, the
  flight is booked, the car is listed. Show the confirmation, not the chat
  transcript.
- **Boring is the point.** If the task looks impressive, viewers credit the
  demo. If the task looks boring and the agent still nails it, viewers
  credit the product.

## Structure the demo

1. State the job in one sentence ("Cancel my three unused subscriptions").
2. Show it happening, sped up if needed, but real.
3. End on the proof: the confirmation email, the refund receipt, the booked
   itinerary. The artifact is the argument.

Keep it under two minutes. Cut everything that isn't the job or the proof.

## Speed is the wow factor

"Wicked fast" and "always-on" were the launch headlines for a reason. When
the capability is table stakes, latency is the differentiator. Demo on the
fastest path, show the timer if you have one, and never apologize for a
loading spinner — remove it instead.

## What not to demo

- Abstract reasoning puzzles. Nobody wakes up needing those solved.
- Multi-step setups. If the demo needs ten minutes of config, the product
  isn't ready to demo.
- Anything you can't show failing gracefully. Live demos break; have the
  recovery be part of the story, not the end of it.
